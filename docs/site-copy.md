### Sovereign Buyer Personas

**Hero subtitle:**
Making complex markets simple. A framework for building personas that drive real targeting, messaging, and content decisions.

**Role/Date/Tags:** Strategy & Frameworks · 2024 · Personas, Strategy, Government

**Metrics:**
- 3 Key Personas — Built for deal consensus

**Persona gallery intro:**

**Section: The Sovereign Buying System**

Sovereign infrastructure deals aren't enterprise IT purchases. Decisions are justified publicly, timelines stretch 7–16 months, and buying committees navigate political risk alongside technical requirements.

Generic personas ("CIO/CTO") collapse under this complexity. You need personas built around power dynamics, career risk, and the language altitude required at each level.

**Key dimensions:**

*The Minister:*
Strategic and geopolitical—not technical-first. Messaging focuses on legacy, sovereignty, and national outcomes.

*The Architect:*
Credibility earned through frameworks, peer references, and clear trade-offs. No hype, just implementation realism.

*The Operator:*
Value demonstrated through ROI and live workloads. Connect sovereignty to operational excellence.

**Section: How These Personas Get Used**

Strategic personas aren't decorative—they're operational tools that drive messaging, content, sales, and deal orchestration.

1. **Messaging Architecture** — Tailor value propositions by altitude—policy for ministers, architecture for technical authorities, economics for operators.
2. **Content Strategy** — Create content formats matched to persona trust signals—briefs for ministers, frameworks for architects, ROI models for operators.
3. **Sales Enablement** — Equip sales teams to navigate buying committees and speak the right language at the right altitude.
4. **Executive Briefings** — Prepare custom briefings that address persona-specific concerns and earning trust at each level.
5. **Long-Cycle Deal Orchestration** — Map the 7–16 month approval journey and orchestrate touchpoints across the buying committee.

**FAQ:**

*Why do sovereign infrastructure deals need different personas than enterprise IT?*
Sovereign deals involve public justification, geopolitical scrutiny, and 7–16 month approval journeys across buying committees. Generic 'CIO/CTO' personas collapse under this complexity—you need personas built around power dynamics, career risk, and the language altitude required at each level (policy/architecture/operations).

*How are these personas actually used in practice?*
These personas drive five key workstreams: (1) Messaging architecture tailored by altitude, (2) Content strategy matched to trust signals, (3) Sales enablement for committee navigation, (4) Executive briefings addressing persona-specific concerns, and (5) Long-cycle deal orchestration mapping the 7–16 month approval journey.

*What makes the Minister persona different from typical 'executive buyer' personas?*
The Minister cares about legacy outcomes—AI competitiveness, sovereignty, GDP impact—not speeds and feeds. They face political fallout, geopolitical scrutiny, and public accountability. Messaging must be strategic and geopolitical, never technical-first. They trust policy briefs, peer nation examples, and top-tier validation—and dismiss vendor bravado.

*Why is the Architect persona described as the 'quiet scapegoat'?*
The Architect translates political ambition into executable architecture but bears technical risk if the recommendation fails. They're caught between political pressure and technical reality. They trust evaluation frameworks, peer references, and implementation realism—and dismiss hype. Winning requires acknowledging complexity, not oversimplifying it.

*How do you sell to the Sovereign Cloud Chief when they're competing with hyperscalers?*
The Operator owns P&L, GPU utilization, and customer satisfaction. They need ROI in 12–18 months, live workload proof, and low-disruption migration plans. Messaging must connect sovereignty to operational excellence and competitive advantage. Sovereignty talk without operational proof gets dismissed immediately.

*Can I use this persona framework for non-sovereign infrastructure deals?*
The framework—building personas around mandate, risk, trust signals, and language altitude—applies to any complex B2B buying committee. The specific sovereign context (geopolitical risk, public justification, national outcomes) is unique, but the methodology works for enterprise infrastructure, defense, healthcare systems, or any multi-stakeholder deal.

*Do you build these personas for clients, or teach teams to build them?*
Both. For fractional engagements, I typically build the initial persona set through stakeholder interviews and deal forensics, then train the team to maintain and evolve them. The goal is to leave you with both the artifacts and the capability to update personas as your market evolves.

### 10-Touch Sales Play

**Hero subtitle:**
Turning cold outreach into executive conversations. A systematic approach to multi-channel prospecting that consistently books meetings.

**Role/Date/Tags:** Sales Engineering · 2023–2024 · Outbound, SDR Playbook, Messaging

**Metrics:**
- 42% Meeting Rate — Cold outbound to VP+
- 25 Days — Sequence duration
- 10 Touchpoints — Across 3 channels

**Section: The Problem with Cold Outreach**

Most SDR teams burn through TAM with generic "just bumping this" emails. The conversion rates exist, but they're built on volume, not strategy.

When you're targeting executives at enterprise accounts, you can't afford to burn bridges. You need a sequence that earns the right to ask for time.

**Three principles:**

*Value First:* Every touch must provide value before asking for anything.

*Multi-Channel:* Be where they are. Email, LinkedIn, Phone, Video.

*Patience Pays:* 25 days allows for trust-building, not just pestering.

**Section: Sample Email Structure**

The "Day 13" email—a value-led touch that references recent news without being salesy.

```
Subject: {FirstName}, saw the news about {CompanyNews}

Hi {FirstName},

Saw the announcement regarding {CompanyNews}—congrats to the team.

Typically when companies in {Industry} make this move, the next challenge becomes {SpecificProblem}.

We recently helped {Competitor} solve this by {Solution}, which led to {Result}.

Thought this case study might be relevant as you navigate the next quarter:
[Link to Case Study]

No ask today, just thought this might be helpful context.

Best,
{MyName}
```

**FAQ:**

*Why 25 days?*
Executive attention is scarce. A 25-day sequence shows persistence without desperation. It gives them time to digest your content and recognize your name before you push for the meeting.

*Does this work for all industries?*
It works best for complex B2B sales where the ACV justifies the effort. If you're selling a transactional $50/mo tool, this is overkill. If you're selling $50k+ outcomes, this is the baseline.

*How much personalization is required?*
The 10-20-70 rule: 10% on the individual (LinkedIn bio), 20% on the company (news, 10-K), 70% on the persona pain points. You don't need to write a novel, just show you've done your homework.

### 120-Day Content Journey

**Hero / Page title:** 120-Day Content Journey

**Case study context:** How we engineered $4M in quarterly pipeline at Cylance. A 120-day content system that turned awareness into closed deals.

**Metrics:**
- $4M Quarterly Pipeline
- 120 Days
- 3 Stages

**Section: The Problem with Random Acts of Content**

Most marketing teams create content based on what they think is cool, not what the buyer needs. This leads to "random acts of content"—blog posts, webinars, and whitepapers that don't connect to a larger strategy.

To engineer demand, you need to map content to the buyer's psychological state at each stage of the journey.

**Three-stage framework:**

*Problem Awareness:* Validate their pain. Show them they aren't crazy for struggling.

*Solution Education:* Teach them how to solve it. Methodology before product.

*Vendor Selection:* Prove you are the best partner to execute that methodology.

**Section: Sample Content Asset**

A "Problem Awareness" LinkedIn post structure that validates pain without pitching product.

```
The hardest part of [Job Function] isn't [Task A].
It's [Hidden Pain Point B].

Most people think success looks like:
- Metric 1
- Metric 2
- Metric 3

But in reality, you're judged on:
- [Political Reality 1]
- [Internal Struggle 2]

If you're feeling [Emotion], you aren't doing it wrong.
The playbook just changed.

Here's how top 1% [Role] are adapting:
1. Step 1...
2. Step 2...
3. Step 3...

No tool can fix this. It's a mindset shift.
```

**FAQ:**

*Do I need all 120 days?*
The timeline is symbolic of a quarter. You can compress it to 30 days for a sprint or expand it to 6 months for enterprise deals. The physics of the journey remain the same.

*What if we don't have a content team?*
You don't need a team. You need a subject matter expert and a writer (or a very good AI workflow). Quality of insight > quantity of production.

*How do we measure this?*
Early stage: Consumption and Qualitative Feedback (comments, DMs). Mid stage: Website engagement and retargeting pool growth. Late stage: Demo requests and pipeline influence.

### It's Good to Be Pitched

**Core concept overview:**

Most agencies sell "the best creative." This concept sells something simpler: the *feeling* you get when you're pitched great ideas. The story follows a marketing exec through three pitches, then lands on the satisfaction of choosing the perfect fit.

*Client context: Juxt Interactive — agency concept / portfolio piece*

**Core insight (blockquote):**

"The experience of being pitched great creative ideas is itself valuable and enjoyable."

Agencies aren't just vendors—they're providers of a premium "creative showcase" experience.

**The Brief:**
- Create a commercial concept in a single session
- Protagonist: Asian woman executive with black-framed glasses
- Three distinct pitching teams (tech, agency, creative collective)
- 30-second format
- Tagline: it's good to be pitched

**Storyboard Frames:**

1. **The Setup** (0:00–0:05) — Wide establishing shot: exec at the head of the table as three distinct teams prepare to pitch. *Mood: anticipation*

2. **Tech Team Presents** (0:05–0:08) — A tech-forward presenter uses a laser pointer to pitch motion and digital concepts. *Mood: energetic*

3. **Executive Reaction (Interested)** (0:08–0:11) — Close-up: thoughtful evaluation—hand on chin, engaged but reserving judgment. *Mood: thoughtful*

4. **Classic Agency Presents** (0:11–0:14) — A polished agency lead draws a strategy framework on a whiteboard. *Mood: confident*

5. **Creative Collective Presents** (0:14–0:17) — High-energy pitch: dual presentation with boards and screen—unexpected, bold ideas. *Mood: electric*

6. **The Eureka Moment** (0:17–0:21) — Clarity arrives—glasses adjustment, then a decisive raised finger. *Mood: clarity*

7. **Mission Accomplished** (0:21–0:24) — Satisfied and resolved—leaning back with a confident smile. *Mood: satisfied*

8. **Tagline Card** (0:24–0:30) — Title card: it's good to be pitched — Juxt Interactive. *Mood: resolution*

**Process:**

1. **Concept Development** (Claude) — Structured the narrative arc, defined the protagonist, and mapped the three pitch styles to create emotional progression. *What it solved: Clear beat structure prevents AI drift*

2. **Technical Specification** (JSON per shot) — Created detailed prompts for each frame with explicit character continuity, camera angles, and mood progression. *What it solved: Character consistency across all frames*

3. **Image Generation** (Nano Banana / Gemini) — Generated storyboard sketches using the JSON specifications, maintaining visual continuity throughout. *What it solved: Sketch style reduces uncanny valley*

**Applications:**

*Client Pitch Deck Asset:* Professional storyboard frames that demonstrate campaign narrative and production readiness.

*Agency Positioning:* Selling the insight: the value isn't just 'the best idea'—it's the experience of having great options.

### "Attribution models break at scale"
*Published: 2026-02-03*

The dirty secret about multi-touch attribution that nobody wants to talk about: it works great when you have 100 leads a month. It breaks completely at 10,000.

The math doesn't scale. The models assume independence between touches. They don't account for brand halo effects. And they definitely can't handle the reality that most B2B purchases involve 6-10 stakeholders who each have their own journey.

What actually works? Focus on incrementality testing and media mix modeling. Accept that you'll never have perfect attribution—but you can still make better decisions.

## 120-Day Content Journey Matrix — Stage Descriptions

**Problem Identification** (Awareness · 2-4 weeks) — Buyer recognizes a business problem or opportunity

**Problem Analysis** (Awareness · 2-3 weeks) — Buyer quantifies impact and urgency

**Internal Consensus** (Consideration · 3-4 weeks) — Building stakeholder alignment on solving the problem

**External Research** (Consideration · 3-6 weeks) — Actively researching solution categories and vendors

**Shortlist & Comparison** (Consideration · 2-4 weeks) — Narrowing to 2-3 vendors for detailed evaluation

**Recommendation / Champion** (Decision · 1-2 weeks) — Champion building internal business case

**Sales Meetings & Demos** (Decision · 2-3 weeks) — Technical and commercial validation

**Purchase Decision** (Action · 2-4 weeks) — Legal, security, procurement review

**Post-Purchase Adoption** (Retention · 30-90 days) — Onboarding, time-to-value, proving ROI

**Renewal** (Retention · Ongoing) — Expansion, upsell, and renewal conversations

### Matrix Row Tooltips

**Buyer Stage:** What the buying team is actually doing—not what marketing wishes they were doing. *How to use: Align content and CTAs to match real buyer behavior, not your funnel stages.*

**User Tasks:** The jobs-to-be-done at this stage. What the buyer is actively trying to accomplish. *How to use: Create content that helps them complete these tasks faster or with more confidence.*

**Players / Personas:** Who's involved at this stage. Influences channels, proof types, and friction tolerance. *How to use: Tailor content format, depth, and messaging to the active persona at each stage.*

**Relevance:** How 'in-market' the buyer is. Determines how aggressive your CTA should be. *How to use: Early stage: educate and build trust. Late stage: enable decision and remove friction.*

**Goal:** The outcome your content must produce at this stage. *How to use: Every piece of content should have ONE clear goal aligned to the buyer's stage.*

**Metric:** How you measure progress and content effectiveness at this stage. *How to use: Track these to understand velocity, conversion rates, and content performance.*

**Content Scoring:** How you translate behavior into intent signals. Scoring should vary by stage. *How to use: Weight actions differently based on buying stage. Late-stage actions score higher.*

**Media:** The format container—blog, webinar, demo, sales conversation, etc. *How to use: Match format to buyer's time investment and information needs at each stage.*

**Strategy:** The message approach—validation, comparison, de-risking, proof. *How to use: Align messaging to the buyer's psychological needs at each stage.*

**Tactics:** Specific executions—retargeting, nurture, SDR enablement, case studies. *How to use: Tactical playbook for each stage. What marketing/sales actually does.*

**User Thoughts:** The cognitive layer—what buyers are thinking at this stage. *How to use: Anticipate these thoughts in your content to build trust and reduce friction.*

**User Feelings:** The emotional layer. Content should reduce anxiety and build confidence. *How to use: Address these feelings explicitly to reduce emotional friction in the buyer journey.*
