Kinds of jean jackets

Type I:
	•	Features: Short, boxy, single chest pocket, belt cinch on the lower back, back pleats, and knife pleats on the front.
	•	Style: Easy to style, pairs well with casual outfits like jeans or trousers.

Type II:
	•	Features: Boxy fit, double chest pockets, no cinch back, and dual waist adjusters.
	•	Style: Often associated with smarter outfits; can be dressed up with a shirt and tie.

Type III:
	•	Features: Longer and slimmer than Type I and II, pointed pocket flaps, V-shaped stitching under chest pockets, and side pockets.
	•	Style: Highly versatile, suitable for both casual and grunge-inspired looks.

Types of Kimono Sleeves
	1.	Furisode Sleeves:
	•	Description: These are the longest sleeves, typically ranging from 80 cm to 114 cm in length. They are associated with young, unmarried women and are used for formal occasions like coming-of-age ceremonies and weddings.
	•	Variations:
	•	Ofurisode (大振袖): The longest, often reaching the ground, typically worn by brides at wedding receptions.
	•	Chufurisode (中振袖): Medium length, around 100 cm, commonly worn for formal events like graduations and weddings.
	•	Kofurisode (小振袖): Shorter, around 80 cm, often used for graduations and other ceremonies.
	2.	Short Sleeves:
	•	Description: These are shorter and more practical, often found on kimonos like Homongi, which are worn by married women for everyday activities.
	•	Use: Suitable for household chores and less formal occasions.
	3.	Traditional Sleeve Lengths:
	•	Pre-WWII: Women’s kimono sleeves varied in length, often longer than those seen today, reflecting age and marital status.
	•	Post-WWII: Shorter sleeves became more standardized due to fabric shortages.
	4.	Kimono Sleeve Construction:
	•	Kimono Sleeves are typically cut as part of the garment, not as separate pieces, which can result in a seamless look at the shoulder.
