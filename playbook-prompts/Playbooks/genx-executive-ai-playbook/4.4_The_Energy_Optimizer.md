# 4.4 Energy Optimizer: Match Tasks to Cognitive State

## What this does
Reviews your schedule and communication patterns to map high-energy times vs. low-value meetings, decision fatigue patterns, context-switching overhead, and recovery time needs. Redesigns your week for sustained peak performance by matching task difficulty to your natural energy rhythms.

## How to use
1. Copy the entire prompt below
2. Paste into ChatGPT, Claude, or your preferred LLM
3. Replace `{{SCHEDULE_DATA}}` with your calendar and activity patterns from the last 60 days
4. Replace `{{COMMUNICATION_PATTERNS}}` with your email/Slack/meeting communication patterns over the same period
5. Hit enter

---

## The Prompt
```
You are an Energy Allocation Analyst tasked with optimizing someone's schedule and work patterns for sustained peak performance. Analyze their schedule and communication patterns to identify inefficiencies and redesign their approach.

<schedule_data>
{{SCHEDULE_DATA}}
</schedule_data>

<communication_patterns>
{{COMMUNICATION_PATTERNS}}
</communication_patterns>

Complete the following tasks in order:
1. Identify peak-energy periods in the schedule and determine whether they're currently occupied by high-impact work or wasted on low-value meetings/activities.
2. Identify decision-fatigue patterns: when does decision quality likely deteriorate over the day, and what activities or meeting types contribute most to that exhaustion?
3. Calculate context-switching overhead: which transitions between work types, meetings, or communication modes are most frequent and most disruptive?
4. Assess recovery time: how much downtime currently exists between high-intensity sessions, and is it adequate given the intensity and frequency of those sessions?
5. Identify specific low-value activities (meetings or tasks) that could be eliminated, delegated, or restructured.
6. Using findings from steps 1-5, design a redistributed schedule that aligns task type to energy state.

Before writing the redesign, work through your reasoning in a scratchpad. Work through, in order: the peak-energy windows and what currently fills them, the decision-fatigue timeline, the most disruptive context switches with a rough frequency count, and the recovery gaps — then, only after that, draft your recommendations.

<scratchpad>
[Work through the four analyses above, in order, then identify low-value activities, before writing the redesign]
</scratchpad>

If the schedule or communication data doesn't contain enough detail to identify a specific pattern (e.g., no timestamps, no meeting-type labels), say so explicitly and state the assumption you're making rather than inventing specific times or frequencies.

Before presenting your final answer, check your own work: confirm every time window, pattern, and recommendation you cite traces back to the provided schedule or communication data, and confirm your response follows the exact five-part structure below.

Present your complete analysis and redesigned approach inside `<analysis>` tags, covering, in order:
- **Energy-Activity Alignment** — specific recommendations for scheduling high-impact work during peak energy periods
- **Decision Load Management** — strategies to batch decisions and minimize decision fatigue
- **Context-Switch Minimization** — concrete changes to reduce disruptive transitions
- **Strategic Recovery Planning** — optimal spacing and duration of recovery periods
- **Low-Value Activity Elimination** — specific meetings or tasks to eliminate, delegate, or restructure

Close the block with `</analysis>` once all five sections are complete.

Do not include your scratchpad in the final response.
```

---

## Pro tips
- Log actual energy levels for at least two weeks (even a rough 1-5 self-rating per time block) before running this — the model can only map peak-energy windows it can see in the data.
- Be specific about meeting types in `{{COMMUNICATION_PATTERNS}}` ("1:1s," "status updates," "external calls") — vague labels like "meetings" collapse everything into one bucket and blunt the context-switch analysis.
- Treat the redesign as a starting hypothesis, not a final schedule — test it for two weeks and feed the results back in as a second pass.
- Pay attention to the recovery-time section specifically if you're back-to-back more than 5 hours a day — that's usually where the most actionable change is.

## Example use case
**An exec who's sharp until 11am then flatlines through back-to-back afternoon meetings:** Feed in six weeks of calendar data plus a description of which meetings tend to run long or feel draining, and get back a redesign that moves the two highest-stakes weekly decisions to the 9-11am block and batches routine status checks into a single Thursday afternoon slot.
