# 3.2 Bias Detector: See Your Blind Spots Before They Cost Millions

## What this does
Examines your last 10 major strategic decisions to identify systematic biases in your process, information you consistently ignore, and which stakeholders' input correlates with success or failure. Builds a custom decision-making checklist to counteract your team's specific blind spots.

## How to use
1. Copy the entire prompt below
2. Paste into ChatGPT, Claude, or your preferred LLM
3. Replace `{{DECISION_HISTORY}}` with a list of your last 10 major strategic decisions, their outcomes, and (where known) who was involved and what information was considered
4. Hit enter

---

## The Prompt
```
You are a Decision Forensics Analyst. Your job is to examine the organization's decision history below and surface systematic biases, ignored information patterns, and stakeholder correlations — using only what's in the history provided.

<decision_history>
{{DECISION_HISTORY}}
</decision_history>

Complete these tasks, in order:

1. Work through each decision in the history individually (see reasoning step below).
2. Identify recurring **systematic biases** across the decisions — e.g., confirmation bias, anchoring bias, groupthink, overconfidence bias, availability bias, sunk cost fallacy — citing which specific decisions exhibit each pattern.
3. Identify **consistently ignored information** — data sources, functions, or perspectives (market research, financial projections, competitive intelligence, technical feasibility, regulatory input, past lessons) that were systematically underweighted across multiple decisions.
4. Identify **stakeholder input correlation** — which stakeholders' involvement (or exclusion) correlates with success vs. failure across the decisions provided.
5. Build a **Decision-Making Checklist** that directly counters the specific blind spots you found in steps 2-4 — not a generic best-practices list.

Before writing your final analysis, reason through this systematically in the scratchpad below: go decision-by-decision, noting outcome, information considered vs. ignored, and stakeholders involved; then look across decisions for the recurring bias, information, and stakeholder patterns; only then draft your checklist.

<scratchpad>
[List each decision with outcome, information used/ignored, and stakeholders involved. Then identify cross-decision patterns for biases, ignored information, and stakeholder correlation before drafting the checklist.]
</scratchpad>

Uncertainty handling: if the decision history doesn't include enough detail on a given decision's outcome, information sources, or stakeholders to support a specific bias or correlation claim, say so explicitly rather than inferring a pattern that isn't actually supported by the input.

Before presenting your final answer, self-check: confirm every bias, ignored-information pattern, and stakeholder correlation you cite references specific decisions from the provided history (not generic assumptions), and confirm each checklist item maps back to one of the blind spots identified above.

Output format, in this order:
1. **Systematic Biases in Decision Process** — each bias named, with the specific decisions it shows up in
2. **Consistently Ignored Information** — each information type, with the decisions where it was missing
3. **Stakeholder Input Correlation Analysis** — patterns of inclusion/exclusion tied to outcomes
4. **Decision-Making Checklist** — specific questions, required information sources, mandatory consultations, process steps, and red flags, each traceable to one of the findings above
```

---

## Pro tips
- Include outcomes (not just decisions) in `{{DECISION_HISTORY}}` — without a clear success/failure label per decision, the model can't correlate anything and will (correctly, per the uncertainty rule) tell you so.
- If you only have 5-6 decisions instead of 10, say so in the input — the model will scale its confidence language accordingly rather than pretending it analyzed a full sample.
- Run this once a year against the prior year's major calls; comparing checklists year over year shows whether you're actually closing the blind spots or just relabeling them.
- Push back if a "bias" reads generic — ask "which specific decision above shows this pattern?" and expect a direct citation, not a hand-wave.

## Example use case
**Post-mortem on a product line's launch decisions:** A CPG exec feeds in the last 10 major product and pricing calls with outcomes and who was in the room for each — the model flags that R&D's technical-feasibility concerns were overridden in 4 of the 5 failed launches, and builds a checklist requiring a documented R&D sign-off before any launch decision goes to the exec committee.
